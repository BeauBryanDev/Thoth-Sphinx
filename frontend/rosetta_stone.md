# The Rosetta Stone: The Broken Slab That Gave Ancient Egypt Its Voice Back

The Rosetta Stone is a broken granodiorite slab carved in 196 BCE with one priestly decree in three scripts: hieroglyphic, Demotic and Greek. French soldiers found it at Fort Julien near Rashid (Rosetta) in July 1799. It was handed to Britain under the 1801 capitulation of Alexandria and has been in the British Museum since 1802. Because the Greek could still be read, the stone gave scholars a way into Egyptian writing. Jean-François Champollion made the decisive breakthrough in 1822, building on earlier work by Silvestre de Sacy, Johan David Åkerblad and Thomas Young.

## TL;DR

- **What it is:** The stone is a fragment of a larger stela. It records a decree issued at Memphis in 196 BCE by a council of Egyptian priests honouring the 13-year-old king Ptolemy V Epiphanes. The text is written in two languages, Egyptian and Greek, using three scripts: hieroglyphic, Demotic and Greek. It is made of granodiorite, not basalt, and is 112.3 cm high and weighs about 760 kg.
- **Why it matters:** The parallel Greek text let scholars test their guesses about the Egyptian versions. Young identified the phonetic spelling of "Ptolemy" in the hieroglyphic cartouches. Champollion, drawing on his command of Coptic, showed that hieroglyphs mix sound-signs with meaning-signs. His *Lettre à M. Dacier* (read on 27 September 1822) and *Précis* (1824) founded modern Egyptology.
- **What to treat with care:** The famous "Je tiens l'affaire!" scene, with Champollion collapsing for five days, is a family tradition first printed decades later. Its wording, location and length vary between versions, so this page presents it as a story, not a documented fact. The credit dispute between Champollion and Young, and the debate over returning the stone to Egypt, are both still live.

## Discovery: A Find in a Fort Wall

### Napoleon's expedition and the savants

In 1798 Napoleon Bonaparte invaded Egypt, which was then part of the Ottoman Empire. He aimed to dominate the eastern Mediterranean and threaten Britain's route to India. Unusually, the army brought a large team of scholars and scientists, the *savants* of the Commission des Sciences et des Arts, who surveyed and recorded the country.

### July 1799 at Fort Julien

In July 1799, French soldiers were strengthening an old Mamluk fort near the port town of Rashid, which the French called Rosette and renamed Fort Julien. They pulled an inscribed slab out of a wall that was being demolished. The officer in charge, engineer Pierre-François Bouchard (1771–1822), saw that it mattered.

The exact day is uncertain. The British Museum says accounts of the discovery "are now rather vague." It notes that the most generally accepted version puts the find on 15 July 1799, when the stone "had apparently been built into a very old wall." Michel-Ange Lancret's report on the find to the Institut d'Égypte in Cairo is dated 19 July 1799, and many sources give that date instead. Some accounts say that a battalion commander, d'Hautpoul, was the senior officer directing the fortification work, with Bouchard leading the works under him.

The *savants* quickly realised the stone's potential. The Greek text ends by ordering that the decree be inscribed "in sacred and native and Greek characters." That meant the three bands were very likely the same text. The stone was sent to Cairo, and copies and prints were soon circulating among European scholars.

### From French find to British possession

The French campaign collapsed. After British and Ottoman forces besieged Alexandria, the French commander, General Jacques-François Menou, capitulated in late August and early September 1801. Article 16 of the Capitulation of Alexandria declared that "the Arabian manuscripts, the statues, and the other collections which have been made for the French Republic, shall be considered as public property, and subject to the disposal of the generals of the combined army."

Menou tried to keep the stone by claiming it as his private property, but the British commander, John Hely-Hutchinson, insisted on it. Colonel Tomkyns Hilgrove Turner took charge of the stone. He later claimed he had seized it personally, though other accounts say a French officer surrendered it in the streets of Alexandria. Turner escorted it to England aboard the captured French frigate *Égyptienne*, arriving at Portsmouth in February 1802. George III presented it to the British Museum in July 1802. The floors were not strong enough at first, so the stone and other sculptures sat in temporary structures in the Museum grounds.

Painted inscriptions still on the stone's sides read "Captured in Egypt by the British Army 1801" and "Presented by King George III." They are a visible record of the Anglo-French imperial rivalry through which it reached London.

### Two centuries in London

The stone has been on display almost continuously since 1802. The one break came in 1917, when the Museum feared bombing during the First World War. According to the British Museum's own blog, the stone "spent the next two years in a station on the Postal Tube Railway 50 feet below the ground at Holborn," returning in 1919. Today it stands in Room 4, and a touchable replica is in Room 1 (Enlightenment).

### The repatriation debate

Calls for the stone's return to Egypt have grown louder. In 2022, the bicentenary of the decipherment, two petitions circulated:

- Egyptologist and former antiquities minister Zahi Hawass's petition passed 100,000 signatures. He said he hoped to reach one million before formally approaching the British Museum. Hawass argues that Egypt had no say in the 1801 agreement, and he calls the stone "the icon of Egyptian identity."
- A petition organised by archaeologist Monica Hanna, which had 4,200 signatures according to an Associated Press report of 30 November 2022, described the stone as "a spoil of war" and an act of plunder.

The British Museum has responded that Egypt's government has not submitted a formal request for its return. It has also noted that most known copies of these priestly decrees remain in Egypt: by its count, 22 of the 29 such decree stelae discovered so far. Opponents of repatriation, such as historian David Abulafia, argue that the stone's history is not only Egyptian and that it belongs in a free "universal museum." Supporters see its presence in London as a legacy of colonial-era seizure.

**Current status:** As of late 2026, the stone remains in the British Museum. The debate is political and ethical as much as legal, and this page does not try to settle it. Readers should know that both positions are held by serious people.

## Physical Characteristics

### Granodiorite, not basalt

For much of the 19th and 20th centuries the stone was described as black basalt. Some institutions still repeat this; the European Space Agency's Rosetta mission FAQ, for example, calls it "a slab of volcanic basalt." The error had a practical cause. After the stone arrived in London, its inscriptions were filled with white chalk to make them easier to read, and the surface was coated in carnauba wax to protect it from visitors' fingers. That darkened the stone.

When conservators cleaned the stone in 1999, they revealed its original dark grey tint, its crystalline sparkle and a pink vein running across the upper left. Geologists Andrew Middleton and Dietrich Klemm then studied it. In "The Geology of the Rosetta Stone" (*Journal of Egyptian Archaeology* 89, 2003), they concluded that it "is a granodiorite, similar in composition to the so-called 'black granite' from Aswan but somewhat finer-grained than most examples of that rock." They added that it "is most probable that the slab of rock used for the Rosetta Stone was obtained from the Ptolemaic quarrying sites to the south of Aswan." They also noted that the French description of it as "une pierre de granite noir" had been essentially correct.

### Size, shape and condition

- **Dimensions:** 112.3 cm high at the tallest point, 75.7 cm wide and 28.4 cm thick.
- **Weight:** about 760 kg.
- **A fragment:** The top and the lower right corner are broken away, and the hieroglyphic section has suffered most. No other pieces were found at Rosetta. Richard Parkinson of the British Museum has estimated that the complete stela stood about 149 cm tall. Reconstructions based on copies of the same kind of decree suggest a rounded top, probably carved with a winged sun disc and scenes of the king with the gods.
- **The back:** The back is rough and unpolished, which suggests the stela was meant to stand against a wall.

### How the text is laid out

The decree runs in three horizontal bands:

1. **Top: hieroglyphs.** 14 lines survive, all incomplete. This is only a fraction of the original text.
2. **Middle: Demotic.** 32 lines, the best preserved of the Egyptian versions.
3. **Bottom: Ancient Greek.** 53 or 54 lines in uncial capitals (the British Museum's own pages give both figures).

Because none of the three versions is complete, scholars have used other copies of the decree to fill gaps. These include stelae found later at sites such as Elephantine, Tell el-Yahudiya and Nobaireh.

## Historical Context: A King, His Priests and a Country in Revolt

### The Ptolemaic world

After Alexander the Great conquered Egypt in 332 BCE, a Macedonian Greek dynasty founded by his general Ptolemy ruled the country from Alexandria for nearly three centuries. Greek became the language of the court and administration. Most Egyptians continued to speak Egyptian, and the temples remained powerful centres of wealth, learning and legitimacy.

### Ptolemy V Epiphanes: a child king in a crisis

Ptolemy V came to the throne as a small child in 204 BCE. Court conspirators had his mother, Arsinoe III, murdered, and regents ruled in his name amid palace intrigue and riots in Alexandria. Meanwhile, the "Great Revolt" swept the country. In the south, native rulers (Horwennefer and then Ankhwennefer) held Thebes and Upper Egypt from about 206 BCE until 186 BCE. Revolts broke out in the Delta as well.

His formal Egyptian coronation at Memphis, the ancient pharaonic capital, was delayed for years. When it finally happened, the priests of Egypt gathered there. According to Egyptologist Foy Scalf of the Oriental Institute (University of Chicago), the synod convened on 27 March 196 BCE, the day after the coronation, and issued the decree on the first anniversary of the king's accession. The king was about 13 years old.

### What the decree says

The decree is a political bargain between the crown and the priesthood, set out in formal language.

**What the king grants:**
- Renewed support for the temples and higher stipends for priests.
- Tax remissions, and limits on what priests pay on entering the priesthood.
- Release from the priests' annual journey to Alexandria.
- An end to forced recruitment for the navy.
- Amnesty for prisoners, and generous treatment of sacred animal cults.

**Recent events it records:**
- The king's siege and capture of the rebel stronghold of Lycopolis in the Delta, presented as proof that he restored order.

**What the priests grant in return:**
- They affirm Ptolemy V's royal cult.
- They order statues of "Ptolemy, protector of Egypt" to be set up in temples and tended three times a day.
- They establish festivals for the king's birthday and accession day.
- They order the decree to be inscribed on stelae of hard stone in "sacred, native and Greek characters" and set up in temples of the first, second and third rank.

The British Museum stresses that the text is formulaic. Much of it repeats wording first used about a century earlier, with names and dates changed, yet it also records real events of the Great Revolt. The Rosetta Stone was one of many mass-produced copies. It belongs to a series of Ptolemaic "synodal" or sacerdotal decrees, including the Canopus Decree (238 BCE) and the Raphia Decree (217 BCE). The stone found at Rosetta was probably first set up in a temple elsewhere, perhaps at Sais, and later reused as building material.

## Three Scripts, Two Languages

The stone records two languages, Egyptian and Greek. Egyptian is written in two different scripts, giving three versions in all.

### Hieroglyphs: the sacred script

The Greeks called this script *hieroglyphika grammata*, "sacred carved letters." By 196 BCE hieroglyphs had been used for about 3,000 years, mainly on temple walls, tombs and monuments. The British Museum describes them as "the sacred symbols suitable for a priestly decree." The language of the hieroglyphic text is itself archaic: a learned imitation of classical Middle Egyptian, much as a modern document might be drafted in formal Latin.

### Demotic: the everyday script

Demotic, from the Greek for "popular," developed from the cursive hieratic script around the 7th century BCE. It was the script of contracts, letters and administration in daily life. It is so abbreviated that its link to hieroglyphs is hard to see at a glance. The Greek text calls it the "native" script, and the British Museum glosses Demotic as "language of the people."

### Ancient Greek: the language of the rulers

Greek was the language of the Macedonian court and the royal bureaucracy. It is the version scholars could read immediately, and the first complete translation of the Greek was published in 1803.

### How the versions relate

The three versions are not word-for-word copies. They are parallel versions of one decree. Scholars still debate which was drafted first; many think Greek or Demotic was the working draft, with the hieroglyphic version a formal rendering. For decipherers, the key point was that the content matched. Royal names such as Ptolemy, which in hieroglyphs were enclosed in oval cartouches, could be located in all three scripts.

## The Decipherment: From Guesswork to System

### Before the stone

The ability to read hieroglyphs disappeared after the end of the 4th century CE. The last known hieroglyphic inscription is a graffito at the temple of Philae dated 394 CE, and the last known Demotic text there dates to 452 CE. For centuries afterwards, scholars followed late classical writers such as Horapollo in treating hieroglyphs as mystical symbols of ideas, not as a way of writing a language.

There were partial insights. Medieval Arab scholars such as Ibn Wahshiyya recognised that some signs might be phonetic, although how accurate they were is disputed. In the 17th century the Jesuit Athanasius Kircher studied Coptic and argued that it descended from ancient Egyptian. In 1762 Jean-Jacques Barthélemy suggested that cartouches enclosed royal or divine names. In 1797 the Danish scholar Georg Zoëga suggested that some signs were phonetic.

### First steps: Silvestre de Sacy and Åkerblad (1802)

Early work focused on the Demotic, the best-preserved Egyptian text. In 1802 the French orientalist Antoine-Isaac Silvestre de Sacy identified several proper names in it. That same year his former student, the Swedish diplomat Johan David Åkerblad, published a Demotic "alphabet" of 29 letters drawn from Greek names; by one count (Wikipedia's article on Åkerblad) he found the correct sound value for 14 of the 29 signs, though other tallies differ by one. Both men assumed Demotic was purely alphabetic, and that wrong assumption stalled their progress.

### Thomas Young: the English polymath (1814–1819)

Thomas Young (1773–1829) was a physician and physicist, best known for his wave theory of light. He began working on the stone in 1814 and made several important observations:

- **Demotic:** He saw that Demotic signs derived from hieroglyphs, and that Demotic mixed phonetic and non-phonetic signs.
- **Ptolemy:** He showed that the cartouches on the Rosetta Stone spelled the name Ptolemy at least partly phonetically, and assigned approximate sound values to its signs.
- **Other readings:** He made progress with the name Berenice, the feminine ending, and many word groups.

He summarised this work in an anonymous article, "Egypt," in the 1819 *Supplement to the Encyclopaedia Britannica*.

The British Museum summarises his limits: in a letter of 1818, the meanings he proposed for sign groups "are mostly correct, but he was unable to analyse how the signs conveyed their meaning." Crucially, he believed phonetic hieroglyphs were used only to write foreign names.

### Jean-François Champollion: the making of a decipherer

**Early life:**
- Born on 23 December 1790 in Figeac, in south-western France, the youngest child of a bookseller.
- Largely raised and educated by his elder brother, Jacques-Joseph Champollion-Figeac, in Grenoble.
- Came to the notice of the prefect of Grenoble, the mathematician Joseph Fourier, who had served on Napoleon's Egyptian expedition.
- At 16, read a paper to the Grenoble academy arguing that the language of ancient Egypt survived in Coptic.

**Languages:**
- Studied in Paris at the Collège de France and the École des Langues Orientales, partly under Silvestre de Sacy.
- Worked on Hebrew, Arabic, Syriac, Chaldean, Persian and above all Coptic.
- Coptic, the last stage of the Egyptian language, written in an alphabet adapted from Greek and preserved in the liturgy of the Coptic Church, was the tool Young lacked. It let Champollion hear the sounds behind the signs and check the meanings of words.

**Career and politics:**
- A fervent supporter of Napoleon who held radical views.
- Lost his post at Grenoble after the Bourbon Restoration and spent time in exile in Figeac.
- Moved to Paris in 1821.

### The path to 1822

Champollion's thinking changed sharply between 1821 and 1822:

- **April 1821:** His short Grenoble publication on hieratic correctly argued that hieratic was a simplified form of hieroglyphs. It also wrongly claimed the Egyptian scripts were "signs of things and not of sounds." He apparently tried to withdraw the text later.
- **1821–1822:** He studied Young's *Britannica* article, which by his own admission he read in Paris.
- **January 1822:** He received an accurate copy of the obelisk from Philae, removed by William Bankes. Its Greek base mentions Ptolemy and Cleopatra, and Bankes had pencilled the identification "Cleopatra" beside one of its two cartouches. Comparing the Ptolemy and Cleopatra cartouches let Champollion assign consistent sound values (P, T, O, L, E and others) and recognise homophones, meaning different signs with the same sound. Young's own copy of the obelisk contained an error that threw him off.
- **The Dendera zodiac:** Champollion read a cartouche in the published drawing of the zodiac ceiling as the Greek imperial title *autokrator*. That reading appeared to date the monument to the Greco-Roman period, against those who claimed it was immensely ancient. Young had read the same cartouche as "Arsinoe." There is a twist. When Champollion reached Dendera in 1828, he found the cartouche he had read was not on the monument at all; according to the Adkinses' account, the Commission's draughtsmen had added it to their drawing. Today the zodiac is generally dated to the late Ptolemaic period.

**14 September 1822:** Champollion examined copies of cartouches from the temple of Abu Simbel made by the architect Jean-Nicolas Huyot. These were names of native pharaohs, not Greek rulers.

- **Ramesses:** One cartouche began with a sun disc, which he knew from Coptic as *Ra* (Re). It ended with the sign he already knew as *s*, and had an unknown sign in the middle. He read it as *Ra-m-s-s*, Ramesses. He linked the middle sign to the Coptic *mise*, "to give birth."
- **Thutmose:** A second cartouche began with an ibis, the sacred bird of the god Thoth, followed by the same middle sign. He read it as *Thot-m-s*, Thutmose.

These readings meant phonetic spelling was not a late Greek-era addition. It had existed in the pharaohs' own names centuries earlier. Champollion had one detail wrong: the middle sign actually stands for two consonants, *ms*, not just *m*. Karl Richard Lepsius later corrected that kind of error, showing in 1837 that many signs stand for two or three consonants.

### The Lettre à M. Dacier (1822)

Champollion's letter to Bon-Joseph Dacier, permanent secretary of the Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres, is dated 22 September 1822. He read it to the Académie on Friday 27 September 1822 and published it in October as *Lettre à M. Dacier relative à l'alphabet des hiéroglyphes phonétiques*.

**What the letter contained:**
- A table of hieroglyphic and Demotic phonetic signs with Greek equivalents.
- Readings of the names of many Greek and Roman rulers of Egypt.
- His own name, written playfully in Demotic.
- His argument that phonetic writing in Egypt was ancient. Richard Bennett quotes the letter in translation: "I believe that the phonetic writing existed in Egypt at a far distant time; that it was just a necessary part of the ideographic (pictorial) script."

**What it left out:** He did not publish the Ramesses and Thutmose readings, which he was still testing.

Thomas Young was in the audience and was invited to sit beside Champollion during the reading. The British Museum calls the public reading of this letter "the moment of decipherment," and its curator Ilona Regulski writes that it "marked the birth of Egyptology."

### The key insight: a mixed writing system

Champollion's decisive step, set out fully in the *Précis du système hiéroglyphique des anciens Égyptiens* (1824), was to see that hieroglyphic writing combines different kinds of signs:

- **Phonetic signs** that write sounds: one, two or three consonants.
- **Logograms (ideograms)** that stand for whole words.
- **Determinatives:** silent signs placed after a word to show what category it belongs to, such as a god, a place, a man or a movement.

One of his arguments was statistical. He counted 1,419 hieroglyphic signs in the Rosetta text against only 486 Greek words, too many for a purely ideographic script and too few distinct signs for a pure alphabet. In the Demotic word for "Alexandria" he spotted phonetic signs followed by a sign marking it as a place name: a determinative. The British Museum notes that what Young had not realised "was that sign sequences could comprise both phonetic and non-phonetic elements."

### After the breakthrough

- **1824–1825:** In Turin, Champollion studied the papyrus "Royal Canon" of kings, opening up far older Egyptian history.
- **1828–1829:** He finally travelled to Egypt with the Italian scholar Ippolito Rosellini, reading inscriptions on monuments along the Nile. On 1 January 1829 he wrote again to Dacier that his alphabet applied to all the monuments of Egypt.
- **1831:** He became the first holder of a chair of Egyptian archaeology at the Collège de France.
- **1832:** He died in Paris on 4 March 1832, aged 41, after a series of strokes.
- **After his death:** His brother published his *Grammaire égyptienne* (1836) and dictionary.
- **By the 1850s:** Lepsius, Emmanuel de Rougé and others had refined the system enough for full translation of Egyptian texts.

## The "Je tiens l'affaire!" Story: What Is Documented and What Is Legend

### The story as usually told

On 14 September 1822, Champollion is said to have run from his lodgings at 28 rue Mazarine to his brother Jacques-Joseph's office at the Institut de France. There he threw down his papers, cried "Je tiens l'affaire!" ("I've got it!"), and collapsed. Some versions add that he stayed unconscious or bedridden for five days.

### What can be said with confidence

- **The date of the breakthrough** is well established. Champollion examined the Abu Simbel cartouches around 14 September 1822, wrote the *Lettre* between then and 22 September, and read it on 27 September.
- **His exhaustion is plausible and widely reported.** Some scholars suggest the *Lettre* was drafted with heavy help from Jacques-Joseph because of his brother's state, though this is an interpretation, not a documented fact.

### What comes from later biographical tradition

- **No contemporary 1822 document is known to describe the collapse.** The *Lettre* itself is a technical text with no anecdote.
- **The earliest printed sources are biographies.** The 2022 Collège de France bicentenary catalogue attributes the story to Champollion's "first biographer," A. Rochas (*Notices biographiques et littéraires sur Champollion le Jeune et MM. Champollion-Figeac*, 1856). Even there it is phrased conditionally ("ce serait ainsi"). Rochas wrote while Jacques-Joseph was alive, so the story very likely came from the family, though this is an inference.
- **Champollion's nephew gives the fullest account.** Aimé Champollion-Figeac, Jacques-Joseph's son, wrote *Les deux Champollion* (1887). Egyptologist Karine Madrigal has quoted his version. In it, Champollion threw a bundle of papers on his brother's desk, crying "Je tiens mon affaire!", and after a long conversation "un affaissement physique et moral" (a physical and mental collapse) seized him: his legs gave way and he fell into a kind of torpor. ARCE's article by Foy Scalf likewise calls it "a legend recounted by Champollion's nephew."
- **Hermine Hartleben's biography fixed the story in the scholarly literature.** Her German biography, *Champollion: sein Leben und sein Werk* (1906), long the most extensive life of Champollion, retold the scene, and later popular histories drew on it heavily.

### What is uncertain or embellished

- **The words:** Versions give "Je tiens l'affaire!", "Je tiens mon affaire!" or "Je tiens mon affaire, vois!" Both Bennett and the British Museum's own pages use different forms.
- **The place:** Some versions put it at the Institut library or Jacques-Joseph's office. Montpellier Egyptologist Bernard Mathieu places it at the brothers' shared home at 28 rue Mazarine, and calls the exclamation "sans doute un peu légendaire" (no doubt somewhat legendary).
- **The "five days":** This figure does not appear in the passages from Aimé's account quoted above. Reference works present it cautiously; the *Encyclopædia Universalis* introduces it with "la tradition veut" ("tradition has it"). Its precise origin is unclear, and it appears to have spread through 20th-century retellings.
- **Popular errors:** Some popular sites give the wrong date (1819, or 27 September).

### Bottom line for readers

The breakthrough of mid-September 1822 is historical fact. The dramatic dash, the shout and a collapse of some kind come from a family tradition first printed in 1856 and expanded in 1887 and 1906. The precise words, the setting and the five-day faint should be treated as legend.

## Rivals and Credit: The Young–Champollion Dispute

### How the quarrel began

The two men first met at the Académie on 27 September 1822. At first Young was generous. In a letter of 29 September 1822, quoted by his biographer Andrew Robinson, he wrote that Champollion "has lately been making some steps in Egyptian literature, which really appear to be gigantic." He added: "It may be said that he found the key in England which has opened the gate for him... but if he did borrow an English key, the lock was so dreadfully rusty, that no common arm would have had strength enough to turn it."

Young was stung, however, by how little the *Lettre* acknowledged his work. In 1823 he published *An Account of Some Recent Discoveries in Hieroglyphical Literature and Egyptian Antiquities*. Its pointed subtitle presented "the Author's Original Alphabet, As Extended by Mr Champollion." He wrote that he "did certainly expect to find the chronology of my own researches a little more distinctly stated."

Champollion replied furiously in March 1823 that he would "never consent to recognise any other original alphabet than my own." In the *Précis* (1824) he did concede, in Robinson's translation, that Young "was the first to publish some correct ideas about the ancient writings of Egypt" and the first to try, "but without complete success," to give phonetic values to the hieroglyphs in the names Ptolemy and Berenice. Robinson calls this acknowledgement "clearly grudging."

### How historians weigh it today

- **The case for Young:** Young took real first steps. He identified Ptolemy, recognised that Demotic derived from hieroglyphs and that scripts could be mixed, and he largely deciphered Demotic. Robinson argues that Young's 1819 article "reoriented Champollion and set him on the right path." The Irish scholar Edward Hincks made a similar case in 1846. Champollion also did not acknowledge Bankes's identification of the Cleopatra cartouche.
- **The case for Champollion:** He built a system that worked consistently on new names and extended it to native pharaohs. He recognised determinatives, used Coptic to read ordinary words and went on to read whole texts. Young, by contrast, believed phonetic signs were confined to foreign names, and in 1827 he abandoned hieroglyphic work.
- **A dispute shaped by nationalism:** The quarrel was overlaid with Anglo-French rivalry from the start, and partisans on both sides kept it going long after both men died.

The prevailing scholarly view is that Young opened the door and Champollion walked through it and mapped the house. Champollion deserves the title of decipherer and founder of Egyptology. Young deserves more credit than Champollion gave him.

## Significance and Legacy

### Linguistic significance

The decipherment brought back a language that had been unreadable for about 1,400 years. Egyptian has one of the longest recorded histories of any language, running from the earliest writing around 3250 BCE to Coptic, which survives in church liturgy. Reading hieroglyphs, hieratic and Demotic opened up royal inscriptions, religious texts, literature, letters, legal documents and accounts. The Coptic connection proved that the ancient language had not vanished but evolved. It remains central to reconstructing how Egyptian sounded.

### Historical significance

The British Museum calls the decipherment of 1822 "the most important event in the history of Egyptology." Within a few years Champollion was using king lists to show that Egyptian civilisation was far older than classical Greece or Rome. Egyptians could now be heard in their own words, not only through Greek and Roman authors. The decree itself has also become a source on Ptolemaic politics, the Great Revolt and relations between a foreign dynasty and native priests.

### Cultural significance

- **A metaphor:** "A Rosetta Stone" now means any key that unlocks a new field of knowledge.
- **Language software:** The Rosetta Stone language-learning software takes its name from the stone.
- **Space exploration:** The European Space Agency named its comet mission Rosetta. Its lander Philae, which made the first soft landing on a comet nucleus on 12 November 2014, is named after the Philae obelisk that helped Champollion. ESA explains that, as the stone was the key to an ancient civilisation, the mission would help unlock "the oldest building blocks of our Solar System: comets."
- **A museum icon:** The British Museum describes the stone as "one of the most visited objects in the Museum," and the British Museum Press's own description of John Ray's book on the stone calls it "the single most visited object in the entire collection." It is also one of the Museum's most popular objects online.
- **Commemoration:** The 2022 bicentenary brought major exhibitions, including "Hieroglyphs: unlocking ancient Egypt" at the British Museum and events at the Collège de France and the Musée Champollion in Figeac.

## Glossary

- **Cartouche:** An oval ring enclosing a royal name in hieroglyphs. The term comes from the French for a cartridge, which the shape resembles. Cartouches let decipherers locate names such as Ptolemy, Cleopatra and Ramesses.
- **Coptic:** The last stage of the Egyptian language, written in the Greek alphabet plus a few signs derived from Demotic. It is still used in the liturgy of the Coptic Orthodox Church, and was Champollion's key to Egyptian vocabulary and sounds.
- **Decree:** An official order. Here it is a resolution of a synod of priests confirming honours for the king and recording his benefactions.
- **Demotic:** A highly cursive Egyptian script, used from about the 7th century BCE for everyday and administrative documents. It forms the middle text on the stone.
- **Determinative:** A silent sign placed at the end of a word to show its category, such as a god, place, person or action. Champollion's recognition of determinatives was a key insight.
- **Granodiorite:** A coarse-grained igneous rock similar to granite but richer in plagioclase feldspar. The stone is made of it, not basalt.
- **Hieratic:** A cursive form of hieroglyphs written with a brush on papyrus. Demotic developed from it.
- **Hieroglyphs:** The pictorial script of ancient Egypt, from the Greek for "sacred carvings." It was used mainly on monuments and for religious texts, and combines phonetic, word and determinative signs.
- **Logogram (ideogram):** A sign that stands for a whole word or idea, not a sound.
- **Memphis decree:** The name for the decree on the Rosetta Stone, issued when the priests gathered at Memphis for Ptolemy V's coronation.
- **Ptolemaic period:** The rule of the Macedonian Greek dynasty founded by Ptolemy I, from about 305 to 30 BCE, ending with Cleopatra VII.
- **Savants:** The scholars and scientists who accompanied Napoleon's expedition to Egypt.
- **Stela (plural stelae; also stele):** An upright stone slab carrying an inscription or relief, set up for public display.
- **Synod:** A formal assembly, here of Egyptian priests from temples across the country.

## Sources and Further Reading

### Books

- **Richard Parkinson, *Cracking Codes: The Rosetta Stone and Decipherment* (British Museum Press, 1999).** The standard illustrated account by a British Museum Egyptologist. It covers the stone, the decipherment and Egyptian writing, with translations.
- **Richard Parkinson, *The Rosetta Stone* (British Museum Press, 2005).** A shorter object biography.
- **Carol Andrews, *The Rosetta Stone* (British Museum, 1981), and Stephen Quirke and Carol Andrews, *The Rosetta Stone* (1988).** Concise museum publications with translation and facsimile.
- **Andrew Robinson, *Cracking the Egyptian Code: The Revolutionary Life of Jean-François Champollion* (Oxford University Press / Thames & Hudson, 2012).** The first full English biography of Champollion. - **Andrew Robinson, *The Last Man Who Knew Everything: Thomas Young* (2006; revised edition, Open Book Publishers, 2023, open access).** Young's side of the story; chapter 15, "Duelling with Champollion," covers the dispute.

- **John Ray, *The Rosetta Stone and the Rebirth of Ancient Egypt* (Harvard University Press, 2007).** A short, witty account by a Cambridge Egyptologist, notably fair to Young.
- **Maurice Pope, *The Story of Decipherment* (revised 1999).** A classic history of decipherments, including Egyptian; useful for the intellectual background.
- **Jean Lacouture, *Champollion: une vie de lumières* (1988).** A major French biography.
- **Robert Solé and Dominique Valbelle, *The Rosetta Stone: The Story of the Decoding of Hieroglyphics* (2002).** An accessible French account in English translation. Robinson notes that it omits some episodes unflattering to Champollion.
- **Andrew Middleton and Dietrich Klemm, "The Geology of the Rosetta Stone," *Journal of Egyptian Archaeology* 89 (2003): 207–216.** The peer-reviewed study that established the granodiorite identification.

## Caveats

- **Discovery date:** Both 15 and 19 July 1799 appear in reputable sources. The British Museum favours 15 July, while 19 July is the date of Lancret's report.
- **Greek line count:** The British Museum gives both 53 and 54 lines of Greek on different pages.
- **Weight:** Weights are rounded and vary slightly between sources (about 760–762 kg).
- **Decree date:** The precise calendar date of the decree depends on converting Macedonian and Egyptian calendar dates. The British Museum and ARCE give 27 March 196 BCE; some older works say 197 BCE.
- **The eureka anecdote:** It rests on family tradition printed from 1856 onward, not on any known 1822 document. Treat the words, setting and five-day collapse as legend.
- **Credit for the decipherment:** Who deserves credit, and how much, remains partly a matter of interpretation. This page reflects the mainstream view that Young made real early progress and Champollion achieved the decipherment.
- **Repatriation:** The repatriation debate is ongoing. The petition figures cited above date from 2022. Since then, The National (16 July 2026), citing the Egyptian newspaper Al Shorouk, has reported that the 2022 petition "has attracted about 350,000 signatures," and Egypt Independent, citing Al-Ahram, reported "up to 300,000 signatures." Both are second-hand reports, not independently verified counts.
